Under the Canopy

How airborne lidar shows a Maya town through the forest, and how I checked whether it was a new find. It wasn't, and that check is the point of this story.

Location withheld. Coordinates, basemap and roads are left out on purpose, as for every archaeological site I map. The view is a local grid, a strip of lidar about 300 m wide, with north up. Region: the Puuc–Chenes hills of Campeche, Mexico.
Step 1 of 6 · The forest

From the air it is all trees

This is the top of the forest as the lidar saw it in 2013: every return that bounced off a leaf or branch. Canopy here reaches about 17 m. A photograph from above would show the same thing, a continuous green surface.

Data: NASA G-LiHT airborne lidar, 2013, 1 m grid.

Step 2 of 6 · Bare earth

Take the trees away

Some laser pulses slip through gaps in the leaves and reach the ground. Keep only those, interpolate between them, and you get a bare-earth terrain model. Lit from the northwest, a leveled hilltop appears, standing about 45 m above the lower ground, with straight-edged shapes on top.

Same block, same scale. Heights raised 1.5× so the shapes read.

Step 3 of 6 · Local relief

Subtract the hill to see what sits on it

A local relief model removes the broad shape of the hill and keeps only what rises above or dips below its surroundings within about 30 m. Long mounds of collapsed masonry turn orange, and courtyards and pits turn purple. The pattern is rectangular courtyards, which nature does not make.

Step 4 of 6 · Sky-view factor

Measure how much sky each spot can see

Sky-view factor asks, for every square metre, how open the sky is around it. Ridges and mound tops are bright, while pits and the bases of walls are dark. It needs no light direction, so edges facing any way show equally. Several dozen small dark pits, 2 to 4 m across, are probably chultunes, underground cisterns.

Step 6 of 6 · The check

Was it already known? Yes: this is Pixoy

The site was missing from the digital site atlas at this spot, and that gap was tempting. But the atlas point for many Chenes sites is off by kilometres. So before telling anyone, I went to the published record.

E. Wyllys Andrews V's 1984 survey describes a hilltop site in the same stretch of hill country as this lidar group. Eric von Euw drew its plan in 1977 for the Corpus of Maya Hieroglyphic Inscriptions. His plan matches the lidar building for building, down to the round structure, his Structure 17.

Von Euw's 1977 plan of Pixoy goes here once the Peabody Museum gives permission to show it. Until then: Corpus of Maya Hieroglyphic Inscriptions, vol. 4, part 1, Pixoy.

The lesson: lidar is very good at showing that something is there, and very bad at telling you whether anyone found it first. Every candidate now gets a literature check (Andrews' reports, the Corpus, regional surveys) before it is called new. I withdrew this one from the list of possible discoveries the same day.
